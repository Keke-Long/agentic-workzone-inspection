Automatic Agentic VLM Inspection of Work Zones Using Connected Vehicle
Data



Abstract
Work-zone traffic control devices must be deployed and maintained in accordance with
context-dependent requirements, yet field inspection is labor-intensive and difficult to conduct
continuously. Connected vehicles can collect synchronized roadway imagery, LiDAR point
clouds, and positioning data during routine travel, creating an opportunity to support recurring
inspection without requiring a dedicated survey for every visit. The challenge is not only to
detect objects, but also to determine which requirements apply to a given work-zone scene,
identify which inspection questions are observable from the available data, coordinate
specialized analysis tools, and preserve the evidence supporting each conclusion. This paper
develops a structure-guided agentic workflow that connects work-zone manuals with multimodal
connected-vehicle data. The manual structure guides regulatory retrieval, while an agent
dynamically selects inspection tasks, plans tool calls, responds to intermediate results or
failures, and assembles evidence-grounded inspection records. The workflow is evaluated on
real work-zone traversals using human-established ground truth at the tool, inspection, and
system levels. Quantitative results and the final inspection scope will be inserted after data
auditing and experiments are completed.
Keywords: work-zone inspection; agentic AI; connected vehicles; multimodal sensing;
retrieval-augmented generation; traffic control devices


1. Introduction
Work-zone safety is a persistent transportation challenge. Work zones were reported to account
for more than 2% of all fatal crashes in the United States each year since 2016 (Finley &
Ullman, 2023). The burden extends beyond fatal events: a work-zone crash occurred
approximately every five minutes nationwide during 2015–2019 (Qian et al., 2024). Crashes
also impose substantial economic losses. The average economic cost of a
property-damage-only crash is estimated at $18,100 in 2024 dollars (FHWA, 2025). These
safety and economic burdens motivate timely inspection of temporary traffic-control facilities. As
work progresses and traffic arrangements change, agencies need to identify deficient,
displaced, or obscured devices and refer potential problems for corrective action.

Conventional work-zone inspection relies on field visits and manual review of imagery and
records. Staff time and travel requirements constrain how frequently geographically dispersed
work zones can be inspected. Geotagged images collected by commercial trucks during routine
travel can support repeated remote review of sign placement, lane configurations, maintenance
closures, and pavement markings (Overall et al., 2026). This establishes a practical opportunity
to use passing vehicles as an information source for inspection. Where instrumented vehicles
also provide synchronized LiDAR and positioning data, their observations may support selected
spatial measurements in addition to visual review.

Existing vehicle-based inspection systems have already automated data collection. Traffic-sign
tracking, tilt and occlusion assessment, and geolocated reporting have been integrated into a
monitoring workflow, with mobile LiDAR used to validate geometric estimates. This workflow
converts roadway observations into actionable asset records for predefined sign-condition tasks
(Dwairi et al., 2026). In a demonstrated work-zone application, an AI experiment screened
images for the presence of a work zone, while the assessment of traffic-control practices still
relied on manual inspection (Overall et al., 2026).

Across engineering domains, there is a shared need to automate the assessment of whether
designs, assets, and site conditions conform to applicable requirements. Automated compliance
checking addresses this need by organizing regulatory requirements, information about the
object under review, and specialized analysis tools into workflows that produce conformance
judgments and review reports. Such workflows have been applied to BIM-based design review
(Peng & Liu, 2023), integrated construction compliance checking (Beach et al., 2024), and
building operation assessment (Beach et al., 2025). LLMs have also been incorporated into BIM
code checking (Madireddy et al., 2025), adaptive design review (Alnuzha & Bloch, 2026), and
construction-site safety monitoring (Chan et al., 2025).

However, automated compliance checking remains underexplored in work-zone inspection
using vehicle-collected data. The vehicle-based studies above do not demonstrate this
capability, while the cross-domain systems address different objects, information structures, and
inspection tasks. In a work zone, a requirement may depend on roadway type, speed, closure
configuration, and work duration. The available observations may reveal a device without
establishing its condition or its spacing relative to another device. Applying the checking
paradigm therefore requires the system to associate the scene with the relevant requirement,
obtain the evidence needed for that particular check, and recognize when the traversal cannot
support a conclusion.

This study develops a structure-guided agentic workflow for that purpose. It links
connected-vehicle imagery, LiDAR, and positioning data with temporary-traffic-control manuals
and specialized analysis tools. Structure-guided retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) uses the
manual's organization of conditions, typical configurations, device requirements, tables, and
notes to narrow the retrieval scope and retain the context needed to interpret a requirement.
The design addresses the possibility that a textually relevant passage is not applicable to the
observed scene. An agent then selects inspection items, coordinates tool execution, and
responds to intermediate evidence or failures. The output connects each inspection status with
its regulatory source and observed evidence, including referral for human review when a
judgment is unsupported.

The paper makes two contributions. First, it develops an agentic work-zone inspection workflow
that combines multimodal vehicle data with structure-guided RAG to produce inspection reports
grounded in traffic-control requirements and observed evidence. Second, it evaluates the
workflow on real work-zone data to assess its accuracy and practical applicability.


2. Methodology

2.1 System Overview
The proposed framework converts connected-vehicle observations into work-zone inspection
records through four stages: layout selection, regulation-grounded inspection planning,
tool-assisted inspection and assessment, and inspection report synthesis (Fig. 1). The sensing
inputs are vehicle-mounted camera imagery and LiDAR point clouds, with the calibration and
synchronization information needed by the analysis tools. Telematics data are not an input to
the framework. A multimodal agent connects these observations with a structured
representation of the Manual on Uniform Traffic Control Devices (MUTCD), 11th Edition, and a
set of scene-evidence tools.
The framework separates deciding what should be inspected from establishing what the
observations can support. Stage 1 selects a source-supported Typical Application (TA). Stage 2
uses structure-guided retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) to identify candidate provisions,
construct explicit inspection requirements, and plan evidence acquisition. Stage 3 executes the
plan and forms evidence-supported judgments. Stage 4 assembles the results into a report that
links each requirement to its normative source, observations, measurements, and assessment
rationale. Evidence-insufficient items remain visible as not_assessed rather than being omitted
or treated as satisfactory.




Fig. 1. Four-stage framework for agentic work-zone inspection.
2.2 Stage 1: Layout Selection
Layout selection establishes the traffic-control configuration that organizes subsequent
regulatory retrieval. The agent uses the available scene context to distinguish features such as
the closed lane, direction of traffic, merging transition, lane shift, and presence of conditional
devices. Known facts are recorded with their provenance; an unknown condition remains
unknown rather than being interpreted as false. Camera observations provide visual context,
while geometric information is used where available and relevant.
The agent searches the MUTCD Typical Applications and examines their source descriptions,
diagrams, and associated notes before selecting the applicable layout. The output is a TA
identifier, the supporting source references, and a scene-based selection rationale. Selecting a
TA narrows the regulatory search space. The selected TA and scene facts are passed to Stage
2 so that both layout-specific and condition-dependent requirements can be considered.

2.3 Stage 2: Regulation-Grounded Inspection Planning
This stage transforms regulatory knowledge into an executable inspection plan. Its
methodological contribution is the explicit connection between the manual's structure,
scene-conditioned requirement activation, and the evidence needed to evaluate each
requirement. It comprises three modules: structured regulatory retrieval, applicable requirement
construction, and evidence acquisition planning.

2.3.1 Structured Regulatory Retrieval
The regulatory representation preserves the MUTCD's source organization, including sections,
Typical Applications, clauses, figure components, tables, notes, and explicit cross-references. A
knowledge graph (KG) connects source units and their relevant relationships, while retained
source locators and text spans allow retrieved information to be checked against the manual.
This representation is constructed independently of the evaluation answers; benchmark
requirement identifiers and expected inspection outcomes are not retrieval inputs.
Candidate generation combines three complementary layers. First, a TA-core layer retrieves
provisions connected to the selected Typical Application, including relevant components and
referenced material. Second, a scene-condition layer activates additional KG candidates using
the recorded scene facts and identified components. Third, a gap-retrieval layer searches for
potentially relevant provisions beyond the graph-derived candidate set. The primary method
uses BM25 for this supplementary search; a dense retriever is a method variant. The two graph
layers supply deterministic candidates, while supplementary retrieval broadens the candidate
pool without replacing the graph-derived provisions.
The candidate set can be expressed as C = C_TA ∪ C_scene ∪ C_gap, where C_TA denotes
the TA-core candidates, C_scene denotes candidates activated by scene conditions, and C_gap
denotes supplementary retrieval results. Candidate retrieval is organized around six inspection
domains: lane-closure layout, advance-warning signs, channelization, transition and taper
geometry, conditional active devices, and the conditional termination area. These domains
bound the current inspection scope; the framework does not claim exhaustive coverage of all
MUTCD provisions.
The structure-guided RAG contribution therefore lies in preserving the relationship between a
retrieved provision, its governing context, and the observed configuration. A textually similar
passage is treated as a candidate, not immediately as an applicable requirement. The
graph-based and supplementary results are passed together to the next module, where
applicability is explicitly resolved and recorded. This separation also permits retrieval coverage
and requirement-selection quality to be evaluated independently.

2.3.2 Applicable Requirement Construction
The agent converts the candidate pool into atomic requirements that can be assessed
separately. For every deterministic KG candidate, it records a structured decision comprising the
activation state (established, false, or unknown), whether the candidate represents an
independently violable requirement, any duplicate source relationship, the retain-or-exclude
action, the decision basis, and the measurement specification. An initial compilation may be
followed by a candidate audit, but unresolved KG candidates must receive a decision before the
requirement registry is finalized.
For each retained item, the system binds the inspected subject, required relation or condition,
threshold where specified, normative modality, applicability conditions, required evidence inputs,
and authoritative source locator and span. Mandatory provisions and guidance retain their
different normative force. Unknown activation conditions remain explicit in the decision record;
retaining an item is not evidence that all of its preconditions have been established. Each
requirement receives a run-local key for linking planning, evidence, and conclusions, while the
MUTCD locator remains the authoritative regulatory citation.
This structured compilation limits the loss of conditions and source context that can occur when
retrieved passages are summarized into free-form inspection questions. It also distinguishes
requirement applicability from observability: a requirement cannot be excluded solely because
the available traversal lacks the evidence needed to assess it. Such limitations are carried
forward to evidence planning and, if unresolved, to the final assessment.

2.3.3 Evidence Acquisition Planning
For each registered requirement, the agent identifies the observations or measurements needed
to support a judgment and checks their availability in the scene data. The planning record
distinguishes information already available, information that is only partially available, and
acquisition that has not yet been attempted. It specifies the relevant tool family, target objects or
frames, required inputs, and the measurement or observation to be returned. No compliance
conclusion is produced during this module.
The plan follows the semantics of the requirement. A device-condition check requires visual
evidence of the relevant device and its condition; a spacing or taper check requires suitable
geometric observations and any inputs needed to instantiate the registered threshold. Detecting
a device alone does not establish its condition, position, or spacing. When an input is
unavailable, the limitation is recorded explicitly rather than replaced with an assumed value.
Stage 2 ends with a source-linked requirement registry and an evidence acquisition plan that
guide execution in Stage 3.

2.4 Stage 3: Tool-Assisted Inspection and Assessment
The agent executes the acquisition plan through the available scene-evidence tools. These
tools expose accepted object observations, image references, device attributes, and geometric
measurements where the sensor data support them. Each returned result is attached to the
corresponding requirement key, together with its provenance, such as the frame, object
identifier, bounding box, measurement inputs, and validity information. The language model
coordinates tool use and interprets returned evidence; numerical measurements are obtained
through the measurement tools rather than invented from the narrative context.
Execution is informed by intermediate results. When evidence is incomplete, the agent can
request further available observations or measurements before finalizing the evidence record.
Tool failures and unresolved inputs are retained as acquisition limitations. This process is
constrained by the available tools and data: repeated requests do not justify assuming that
missing evidence exists. Before assessment, the evidence record accounts for the required
inputs of each registered item, including inputs that could not be obtained.
The assessment then compares the bound evidence with the registered requirement. An item is
satisfied when sufficient valid evidence supports the specified condition, deficient when
sufficient valid evidence supports a departure from that condition, and not_assessed when the
available evidence does not support either conclusion. Numeric checks use the registered
relation, threshold or formula, units, and required contextual inputs. Qualitative checks cite the
observations that support the judgment. An acquisition failure or an unobserved device is not,
by itself, proof of a deficiency.
Although acquisition and assessment form one stage in the conceptual framework, evidence
recording precedes conclusion formation. Each registered requirement receives one final status
with a rationale and evidence references. The assessment preserves the distinction between a
mandatory requirement and guidance, so that a reported departure is interpreted with the
normative force of its source. Requirements and source bindings established in Stage 2 are not
silently rewritten to fit downstream observations.

2.5 Stage 4: Inspection Report Synthesis
The final stage converts the structured assessment records into a readable inspection report.
The report begins with the scene identifier, selected layout, inspection scope, and a summary of
assessed and unassessed requirements. A requirement-level results table presents the
inspected condition, normative class, MUTCD source, outcome, and associated issue or
evidence references. Detailed evidence records provide the supporting images or image
regions, relevant measurements, and the explanation connecting those observations to the
conclusion.
Findings concerning the same physical defect are grouped while retaining requirement-level
conclusions. Items marked not_assessed are listed separately with the missing evidence or
measurement inputs. Report synthesis preserves the finalized judgments and their links to
regulatory sources and evidence.

3. Experimental Design and Results
This chapter reports the completed experiments on Work Zone 01. The evaluation separates
regulatory retrieval and requirement construction from evidence acquisition and final reporting,
following the four-stage organization in Section 2.

3.1 Data Source and Evaluation Reference
The data collection campaign used the laboratory's Level 3 automated driving vehicle at two
work zones in Madison, Wisconsin. The results reported here concern the completed reference
case, Work Zone 01 (scene-CATS-01); the second site is not included in the quantitative results.
Camera images, LiDAR data, and calibration and pose metadata are organized in a
nuScenes-style format. This describes the data structure rather than use of the public nuScenes
benchmark.
Work Zone 01 contains a single left-lane closure on a four-lane undivided roadway, with a
merging taper and an arrow board. The recorded inspection period is daytime, and the
posted-speed input is 30 mph, supplied as a scene fact. The selected layout is TA-30. The
materialized scene contains 3,631 accepted bounding-box observations across six camera
views; these are observations across frames, not 3,631 distinct devices. The current evaluation
uses reviewed observations and confirmed device-condition findings, so it evaluates the
inspection workflow conditional on those inputs rather than an unassisted perception system.
Requirement selection is scored against a frozen, evaluator-only reference containing 15
applicable items: five mandatory provisions and ten guidance provisions. It covers the six
inspection domains defined in Section 2. The reference was produced through a separate
autonomous MUTCD content audit and is not expert-validated ground truth or an exhaustive
requirement catalog. Its identifiers, target count, and expected answers are withheld from the
inspection agent. Candidate recall is the fraction of reference items represented in the retrieved
pool; requirement recall is the fraction matched by registered requirements; activation precision
is the number of matched items divided by the number of registered requirements.

3.2 Compared Methods and Experimental Controls
Five regulatory-access configurations are evaluated: KG only, BM25 only, Dense only, KG +
BM25, and KG + Dense. KG only uses the TA-core and scene-condition graph layers. The
retrieval-only baselines use lexical or dense retrieval without these graph layers. The combined
methods supplement the graph-derived candidates with BM25 or E5-large-v2 retrieval,
respectively. KG + BM25 is the primary method, and KG + Dense tests the alternative
supplementary retriever.
The recorded runs use the same scene context, selected TA, inspection domains, source index,
evaluator reference, and controller model, gpt-5.6-luna with medium reasoning effort.
Supplementary and retrieval-only searches retain up to 18 candidates per domain. Candidate
recall is the common comparison across all five methods. Requirement construction is
compared directly between the two combined methods, whose retained runs include the
structured candidate-decision audit. The retained KG-only run predates that audit and has
seven unresolved high-priority candidate dispositions; its downstream scores are historical
diagnostics, not a matched ablation of the current compiler. Retrieval-only runs also use a
different, free-form registration procedure.
Each configuration has one retained run on this case. The results therefore describe observed
behavior without estimating run-to-run variance or statistical significance. The original software's
retrieval and requirement-registration stages are both part of Stage 2 in the four-stage
framework used in this paper.

only covered four (26.7%). Thus, the graph structure supplied substantially greater candidate
coverage in this case. Because KG only already reached full candidate recall, the experiment

valid source attribution alone does not establish correct applicability. The retained KG-only and

procedures differ from the audited combined runs.

3.4 Evidence Acquisition and System-Level Results
The KG + BM25 pipeline generated availability records for all 19 registered requirements. All
records consistently partitioned required inputs and included an evidence strategy and decision
rule, but only 15 of 19 plans covered all required acquisition-tool families. The planning audit
therefore did not fully pass. During the evidence-acquisition run, 36 evidence records accounted
for all 19 requirements, including four acquisition-failure records. All 23 inventoried traffic-sign
representatives and the single arrow-board representative were inspected, and each of the four
inventoried channelizer classes was sampled. These are coverage measures over the accepted
inventory, not detection accuracy or proof that every field device was inspected.
The original sealed pipeline produced 14 satisfied, two deficient, and three not_assessed
outcomes. Two pending geometric checks were subsequently completed in a documented
evaluation supplement, without changing the normative requirement registry. The resulting
report contains 16 satisfied, two deficient, and one not_assessed outcome (Table 2).
Assessment coverage increased from 16/19 (84.2%) to 18/19 (94.7%); these percentages
measure the availability of a definitive status, not judgment accuracy or overall work-zone
compliance.

The two deficient requirements refer to MUTCD Section 6K.01, paragraphs 14 and 15, and
share one consolidated channelizing-device-condition issue. The supporting findings identify

Geometric evaluation covered 30 channelizer gaps, with no reliable clear exceedance reported
under the registered thresholds. Supplementary sign-spacing estimates for A, B, and C were
270.8, 249.8, and 219.4 ft, respectively, exceeding the registered 100-ft recommendation. The
fitted taper length was 158.7 ft, compared with a registered minimum of 140.9 ft using a lateral
offset of 9.39 ft and a speed of 30 mph. These track-derived values are screening
measurements; no independent survey-based error estimate is available. The remaining
not_assessed item concerns the highway-conditions warning-sign provision in Section 6H.02,
paragraph 1.

3.5 Computational Cost
Table 3 reports resource use for retrieval and requirement construction in the two audited
combined runs; it excludes downstream inspection, data preparation, and supplementary
geometry evaluation. KG + Dense used approximately 1.88 times the tokens and 1.30 times the
wall time of KG + BM25 while achieving the same requirement recall and lower activation
precision. This case supports BM25 as the less expensive supplementary retriever under the
recorded settings, but does not establish a general ranking across work zones.

3.6 Limitations of the Current Evaluation
The completed evidence supports a single-case feasibility study. Generalization across work
zones, repeatability across model runs, and final judgment accuracy remain unestablished.
Reviewed bounding boxes and confirmed condition findings contribute to the current input, and
the requirement reference has not undergone independent expert validation. Object-detection
mAP, condition-classification accuracy, survey-based geometry errors, and human report-quality
scores are therefore not reported.
The final status counts also require caution: several retained rationales assign satisfied because
no clear violation was established while acknowledging unresolved visual details. This falls short
of the positive-evidence criterion described in Section 2 and should be assessed through an
independent conclusion audit. Reporting these outcomes as system outputs, separately from
requirement-retrieval scores and the later geometry supplement, prevents procedural
completeness from being mistaken for validated inspection accuracy.


4. Conclusions
This study develops a structure-guided agentic workflow for converting connected-vehicle
observations into traceable work-zone inspection records. The final conclusion will be written
after experiments establish the reliability of the component tools, the value of dynamic
orchestration, the supported inspection scope, and the principal failure modes.
